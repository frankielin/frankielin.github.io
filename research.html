O Variety, Where Art Thou: Competitive Spillovers in Chain Retail
[+abstract]
National retail chains increasingly dominate the American grocery sector, bringing distinctive operating strategies such as uniform pricing and centralized supply networks to local markets. I use the rapid rise of the dollar store segment to quantify how competitive pressure transmits both within local markets and across retail chain networks. To address endogeneity, I instrument for dollar store growth with national retail bankruptcies in the 2010s, which lowered entry costs by freeing store-sized retail space. While I find that local entry has little effect on incumbent prices, a one standard deviation increase in dollar stores across a firm's network reduces price growth of continuing products by approximately 3.5 percentage points. These results suggest that competition passes through retail chain networks rather than remaining a purely local phenomenon, highlighting how retail structures and strategies shape consumer welfare.
Gone with the Wind: Long-Run Effects of Forced Displacement from General Order No. 11
[+abstract]
In August 1863, General Order No. 11 forced the near-complete evacuation of four western Missouri counties and displaced an estimated 20,000 civilians in one of the largest forced removals in U.S. history. Combining propensity score matching methods with 80 years of linked full-count census records, I use the order to trace the long-tail effects of forced displacement across multiple generations. In the decades immediately following the event, displaced families show little to no difference in economic status relative to matched neighbors a county away. However, in subsequent generations, outcomes diverge. By 1940, descendants of displaced households hold higher occupational income scores and are more likely to have graduated from high school. These gains correspond to a reallocation away from farming and self-employment, alongside persistently higher rates of outmigration and urban residence. Taken together, the findings suggest that displacement severed household ties to agricultural life and redirected subsequent generations toward portable investments in human capital and geographic mobility.
Mail-Ordered Mobility? Intergenerational Impacts of Homeownership
with Zohal Barsi
[+abstract]
This paper studies how an exogenous expansion in access to homeownership shaped the geography of economic opportunity in the U.S. in the 20th century. We examine the rise of mail-order kit housing in the United States between 1910 and 1930, an innovation that lowered construction costs, standardized designs, and tied housing to railroads. Using newly digitized data on kit-housing firms’ mill locations and full-count census records, we exploit spatial variation in transportation costs as a source of exogenous variation in county-level homeownership access. We estimate the contemporaneous effects of increased homeownership on residential tenure, civic engagement, and local institutional development. To complement the county-level analysis, we link novel person-level purchase records from a leading kit-housing firm to census data to study intergenerational mobility cause by access to homeownership.
This Just In: Unemployment Surprises and Their Transmission Through the TV News
with Christopher Tice-Raskin
[+abstract]
This paper examines how local exposure to economic news on television influences labor market outcomes in U.S. media markets. To do this, we construct a novel measure of economic news reporting sentiment using the near-universe of cable news transcripts from CNN, CNBC, and Fox News. We address potential endogeneity in television viewing patterns with a shift-share instrument, exploiting plausibly exogenous variation in the relative reporting slant of each network alongside contemporaneous viewership shares. The results indicate that higher exposure to positive economic news decreases local unemployment rates. Additional analysis shows that greater exposure also increases job separations and hiring rates, consistent with models where adjustments in consumption due to consumer sentiment have downstream consequences local labor markets.
Canned: Markups and the Prison Commissary
with Leah Pomerantz and Zohal Barsi
[+abstract]
Prison commissaries are captive retail markets serving incarcerated consumers whose outside options are sharply constrained. In recent years, prison cost-shifting practices have led to reductions in provisions and an increased reliance on commissary systems. To measure market power, we characterize the structure of commissary markets in the United States by comparing markups on carbonated beverages to their counterparts paid just outside prison walls. Leveraging retail scanner data alongside a novel dataset on commissary prices and quantities, assembled through public records requests, we recover own- and cross-price elasticities. Our findings indicate that markups paid in California prison commissaries are remarkably similar, in absolute terms, to those in outside retail markets. This suggests that, despite wielding relatively higher market power over a captive population, commissary operators do not exploit this market asymmetry.

School Shootings and Academic Achievement: A Look at the 2014 Isla Vista Killings
Undergraduate Senior Thesis
[+abstract]
This study analyzes how one fatal shooting, the 2014 Isla Vista Killings at the University of California, Santa Barbara, affected various student groups, motivated by growing interest in how school-related gun violence impacts surviving students. Using detailed student-level data from several California universities, I estimate impacts at both the extensive margin, by comparing UCSB students to those at other universities using a synthetic control design, and at the intensive margin, by comparing students in victims' social networks to their UCSB peers using a standard difference-in-differences design. Observable outcomes include quarterly grades, course failure rates, and dropout rates. I find that the incident caused an immediate one-quarter grade increase along both margins, but UCSB students' academic performance deteriorated in subsequent quarters. Dropout rates for students in victims' social networks rose substantially, especially among female students.
